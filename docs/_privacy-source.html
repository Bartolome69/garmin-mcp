What this does with your data

You are about to give a stranger’s website your Garmin login. That deserves a straight answer rather than four pages of legal throat-clearing, so here is the whole of it.

Your Garmin password

When you sign in, your email and password are passed straight to Garmin to be exchanged for an access token. The password is never written to disk, never logged, and is dropped from memory the moment Garmin accepts it. It is not kept in any form: not encrypted, not hashed, not at all. Nobody can recover it later, including me.

Garmin turns away sign-ins from data-centre addresses, so the sign-in step goes out through a residential relay in the UK run by IPRoyal. The connection is encrypted between this server and Garmin before it reaches the relay, the same way your browser encrypts a connection to your bank: the relay can see that something is going to Garmin, but not your email, your password or anything Garmin sends back.

If you would rather not type it here at all, that is a reasonable instinct. Run the whole thing on your own machine and your Garmin session never leaves it.

What is stored, and where

One row per person, on a single server in London, run by Fly.io. That row holds:

  • The access token Garmin issued you, encrypted at rest. This is the part that does the work: it is what lets the connector read your training without asking you to sign in again. If you go a week without using it, the server renews this token itself, reading nothing, so a long break does not end your connection.
  • Your email address, so we can get in touch if something goes wrong with your connection, and now and then about Daash, such as new features or plans. The sign-in page says so under the Connect button. Every one of those emails has a link that stops them in one click, and your connection carries on as before. Your address is never shared or used for anything else.
  • A one-way fingerprint of your email. Signing in again has to recognise you in order to retire your previous session, and that is the only thing this is for. It cannot be turned back into your address.
  • Three timestamps, for when the connection was made, last used, and last renewed by the server.
  • Two small markers: which “what’s new” note you have already been shown in a chat, so it is said only once, and whether you have asked for no more update emails.

Nothing from Garmin is stored: no activities, no heart rate, no sleep, no location. (The one thing you can choose to change that is joining a Daash crew, below.) Each question fetches from Garmin fresh and the answer is gone when the request ends. The one exception is speed: your scheduled workouts and workout list are held in memory for up to ten minutes, so a chat about your plan doesn’t refetch them every turn, and are dropped whenever a change is made through Daash. Nothing is written to disk. There is no copy of your training history on this server to leak, subpoena or lose.

Your IP address is held in memory for a few minutes so that repeated failed sign-ins can be slowed down. It is never written to disk, and the web server’s request log is switched off precisely so that private connector addresses do not end up in it.

What it can do inside your Garmin account

Reading is unrestricted. Activities, splits, heart-rate zones, daily summaries, sleep, body battery, weight, personal records, the same things you see in the Garmin Connect app.

Writing is to the workout library: creating a workout, putting it on a date, taking it off again, and deleting one you no longer want. And, only when you ask, a run you recorded can be renamed, given notes, corrected in type or given its shoes. Deleting is gated rather than merely discouraged. The first attempt only reads the workout’s name back, and removing it takes a second one quoting that name, so nothing goes without having been named out loud first.

Your training history stays as recorded. There is no tool that deletes a recorded activity or changes what it measured (its distance, time, heart rate, splits or route), changes your profile, posts anything, or touches anyone else’s account. A run you actually did cannot be lost through this, whatever is asked of it.

Who else sees your training data

This is the part people miss, so it gets said plainly.

Whatever you ask for arrives in your chat.

Once your Garmin data is in a conversation, it is in that conversation, which means Anthropic or OpenAI handle it under their privacy policy, exactly as they handle everything else you type. If you are not comfortable with your resting heart rate sitting in your chat history, this tool is not for you, and no promise I make about my own server changes that.

Sharing a week is up to you. The plan card’s Share button opens a page on this site that draws the week you are looking at as an image, for you to post, send or keep. The week travels in the part of the link after the #, which browsers keep to themselves, so it never reaches this server or any other, and that page has no analytics and stores nothing. It carries only what the image shows: the plan’s name, the dates, and each session’s day, type, name, distance and shape, with the pace or time once it is done. Its “Send Daash to a friend” link is the front page marked as coming from a share, with nothing about you or your week in it. Who sees the image after that is your choice.

Beyond that: nothing is sold, rented, or handed to advertisers, data brokers or anyone else. There is no third party with access to the database. It is one server, one person, no business model.

This website

These pages count views using PostHog on its European servers, configured to leave nothing behind: no cookies, no advertising identifiers, no session recording, and no profile built about you. It records that a page was opened and that a button was pressed, which is how I know whether the instructions actually work. If you use the box on the front page that asks which connector should be next, the name you tapped or typed is recorded the same way, and that is all it carries.

The server reports a few moments the same way: a sign-in that worked, a sign-in that failed and roughly why, the first time a new connection is used, once a day that it was used that day, once a day the name of each tool used (such as “get_plan”, and how many seconds it took to answer, never what it returned), once an hour how long each tool took across everyone, with no one’s name or address on it, when a tool failed in a way it shouldn’t (its name and the kind of error, never the message), when a tool was held back because it was asked for at the same time as the plan card (its name only), when a “what’s new” note was shown, when someone unsubscribed from update emails, and when a connection could not be renewed, by Claude or by Garmin, so it would need connecting again. Each carries a few yes-or-no flags, the app you connected from (such as Claude or ChatGPT) and your email address, so I can see who is using it, which features earn their place, and help if something breaks.

Neither receives your Garmin data or your connector address. That is also why this site has no cookie banner: there is nothing to ask you to consent to.

Daash Crews, only if you join one

A Daash crew is a small group of friends who all use Daash. It shows them each other’s easy and long runs for the week and suggests runs to do together. Nobody is in a crew unless they join one with an invite code, and while you are in one, the crew’s members can see this, and the server keeps it so they can:

  • The name you chose for the crew, and the crew’s own name and invite code.
  • When you usually run, as rough times such as “weekday evenings”, and whether you usually go to the crew’s club run.
  • Your planned easy and long runs for the next two weeks: the day, whether it is easy or long, and the distance. A hard session such as intervals, or any run with 2 km or more of reps, is kept as the word “hard” on its day and nothing else. Each day is deleted once it has passed.

These are read from your own plan when you look at it in Daash, so a crew causes no extra requests to Garmin. Runs you have done, your pace, heart rate, sleep, routes, location and everything else from Garmin are never kept or shown to the crew: crews match people by day and time, never by speed. Only members of your crew can see any of it, through their own Daash, unless whoever started the crew turns on its page (below).

The crew card can turn the crew’s week into a message or an image: a calendar of each member’s chosen name and their easy and long runs by day with distances (a hard session as the word “hard”), the club run, and the invite code, for a member to copy, save or send to WhatsApp. The image is drawn on the member’s own device by the same share page as a plan week, and the week travels in the link the same way, reaching no server. That only happens when they press the button, and it goes to whichever chat they choose; Daash sends nothing itself.

The crew’s page, only if its starter turns it on. Whoever started a crew can make a link to a web page of the crew’s week, for the group to pin in its chat. Daash asks them first, and every member’s crew card says when the page is on. Anyone with the link can see what the crew card shows for this week and next: members’ chosen names, the days they run, easy, long or “hard” with distances, which runs line up and at what time of day, and the club run. It never shows the invite code, pace or anything else. The link is long and random, the page is kept out of search engines and has no analytics or cookies, and the crew’s starter can replace the link, which stops the old one working, or turn it off at any time.

Friends without Daash, on the crew’s page. Anyone with the page’s link can add their easy runs to it without Daash or Garmin. What is kept for them: the first name they type, the times of their usual week they run easy (a day and morning, afternoon or evening), any changes they make to this week or next, and a four-digit PIN, stored scrambled so it can’t be read back, not even by Daash. Their phone remembers them with a random key kept in its browser. Nothing else is asked for or kept: no email, no account, no location. Days that have passed are deleted. After three weeks without them opening the page, their runs stop being matched until they say they’re still running. “Remove me” on the page deletes everything kept for them at once, and whoever started the crew can remove them too.

Leaving the crew deletes all of it at once, from the page too. So does Disconnect. When the last member leaves, the crew itself is deleted.

Getting rid of it

Open the sign-in page and choose Disconnect. That deletes your row outright (token, email, fingerprint, timestamps, markers), and your place in any crew with everything it kept, and the connection stops working immediately. Nothing is retained afterwards, so there is no backlog to request a copy of or ask to be forgotten from.

Signing in again has the same effect on whatever came before it: the old session is destroyed as the new one is created. If you ever think a connection has ended up somewhere it shouldn’t, signing in once more takes it back.

Removing the connector in Claude or ChatGPT is worth doing too, but it only removes it at their end. The Disconnect button is what deletes the stored session here. Nothing changes on Garmin’s side either way.

The bits that are legally dull but true

This is a personal project run by Bart Etcheverry, in the UK. It is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or connected to Garmin, Anthropic or OpenAI. It reaches Garmin through the same private interface the Garmin Connect website uses, which is why it can break when Garmin changes something.

It is not intended for children under 13, and it is not a medical device. Nothing it tells you is medical advice.

If this policy changes, the date at the top changes with it, and the edit history is public like the rest of it. Questions, or anything here that turns out not to match the code: open an issue on GitHub.
